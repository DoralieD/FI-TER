\section{Related Work}

Overcoming medical data silos has driven significant research into synthetic health data generation. Deep generative models, such as medGAN \cite{choi2017}, have been proposed to synthesize discrete electronic health records, while more recent studies provide broader evaluation frameworks for GAN-based synthetic EHR generation \cite{yan2024}. These approaches are statistically powerful and can capture complex distributions from real patient records. However, purely data-driven generative models may struggle to guarantee strict logical and physiological consistency, especially when the generated samples must satisfy clinical constraints across heterogeneous variables. In contrast, our work adopts a rule-based and expert-driven physiological generation pipeline. Rather than learning distributions directly from real EHRs, the proposed generator explicitly encodes medical relationships between vital signs, observations, laboratory biomarkers, imaging indicators, and final diagnoses. This design makes the generated data particularly suitable for validating diagnostic algorithms under controlled and clinically coherent conditions.

Recent literature in medical artificial intelligence also emphasizes the importance of multimodal data fusion in smart healthcare systems. Surveys on multimodal information fusion show that combining heterogeneous sources, such as clinical records, sensor measurements, laboratory values, and contextual medical data, can improve the robustness of healthcare prediction systems \cite{shaik2023}. In the IoMT context, e-health gateways and data fusion architectures are often studied for real-time monitoring and distributed healthcare applications \cite{rahmani2018,ahmed2024}. However, many IoMT-oriented approaches focus on localized temporal analysis, such as detecting abnormalities from sensor time series. Our work differs from these approaches by simulating an emergency triage scenario as a unified tabular snapshot. The generated dataset combines demographic information, vital signs, visual observations, laboratory markers, and specialty-specific examinations into a single multimodal representation corresponding to the clinical state of a patient at triage.

Tabular data remains a challenging domain for deep learning. Although tree-based models have historically dominated tabular prediction tasks, recent work has revisited the role of deep architectures for structured data \cite{gorishniy2021}. In particular, architectures inspired by Transformers can project tabular features into embedding tokens and model non-linear interactions through self-attention mechanisms. Following this direction, our experimental framework compares a baseline multilayer perceptron with a tabular Transformer. The multilayer perceptron represents a classical early-fusion strategy, where all features are concatenated before classification, while the Transformer-based model aims to capture contextual dependencies between medical variables through attention-based feature interactions.

Explainability is another essential requirement in medical decision-support systems. SHAP has become a widely used framework for interpreting machine learning predictions by estimating local and global feature contributions \cite{lundberg2017}. In our work, SHAP is used not only to interpret the trained neural model, but also to validate whether the model relies on clinically meaningful variables. For example, a coherent model should associate cardiac diagnoses with features such as troponin, ECG abnormalities, BNP, or ejection fraction, and infectious diagnoses with markers such as CRP, procalcitonin, lactates, or culture results. Therefore, explainability is used as an additional validation layer for the physiological integrity of the synthetic dataset.

A pervasive issue in medical deep learning is class imbalance, where rare but life-threatening conditions are often underrepresented compared with more frequent or benign cases. Traditional resampling techniques such as SMOTE \cite{chawla2002} attempt to address this problem by interpolating minority-class samples in feature space. However, such interpolation may be problematic in multimodal medical settings because it can generate clinically inconsistent combinations of variables. Instead of applying synthetic oversampling after dataset creation, our approach directly controls the generation of minority classes through the rule-based engine. This allows severe conditions, such as severe sepsis or hemorrhagic stroke, to be generated at scale while preserving physiological plausibility. In addition, the learning pipeline uses cost-sensitive training through a weighted Cross-Entropy loss, encouraging the neural networks to learn discriminative boundaries for critical diagnoses without sacrificing the clinical coherence of the tabular representations.
