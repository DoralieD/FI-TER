\section{Experimental Results}

The evaluation of the proposed multimodal fusion framework was conducted on a 20\% hold-out test set derived from the 500 000 synthetic patient records. The objective of this section is to assess three complementary aspects: the physiological coherence of the generated data, the predictive performance of the benchmarked models, and the interpretability of the learned decision rules.

\subsection{Validation of Synthetic Physiological Coherence}

Before evaluating the predictive models, we first verified whether the rule-based generation engine produced coherent clinical relationships rather than random statistical noise. Figure \ref{fig:corr_matrix} presents a correlation matrix computed on representative physiological and biological variables.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{10_Correlation_Matrix.png}}
\caption{Physiological correlation matrix illustrating inter-variable dependencies within the synthetic IoMT dataset.}
\label{fig:corr_matrix}
\end{figure*}

The observed correlation structure confirms that the generated variables preserve clinically meaningful relationships. For example, inflammatory biomarkers such as \texttt{Leukocytes\_G\_L} and \texttt{CRP\_mg\_L} form a coherent correlation block, while cardiovascular variables such as BNP, LVEF, and Troponin reflect expected dependencies within the cardiology branch. This supports the use of the generated dataset as a structured benchmark for multimodal medical classification.

\subsection{Predictive Performance}

Figure \ref{fig:perf_global} summarizes the main classification metrics obtained by the MLP baseline and the Tabular Transformer on the test set.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{01_Global_Performances.png}}
\caption{Global performance comparison between the MLP baseline and the Tabular Transformer.}
\label{fig:perf_global}
\end{figure}

The Tabular Transformer achieved the highest global accuracy, reaching 77.1\%, compared with 75.2\% for the MLP. However, the MLP achieved better precision and Macro F1-score, with 66.5\% precision and 63.5\% Macro F1-score, compared with 59.1\% and 60.8\% for the Transformer. This indicates that the Transformer better captures majority-class patterns, while the MLP appears more conservative and more reliable on minority diagnostic classes. Both models obtained similar recall values, showing that the class-weighted training strategy helped reduce the impact of class imbalance.

\subsection{Precision-Recall Analysis for Imbalanced Classes}

Because the dataset contains imbalanced diagnostic classes, the Precision-Recall curve provides a more informative evaluation than ROC-AUC alone. Figure \ref{fig:pr_curve} presents the micro-averaged Precision-Recall curves for both architectures.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{09_Precision_Recall_Curve.png}}
\caption{Precision-Recall curve for evaluating robustness on imbalanced clinical classes.}
\label{fig:pr_curve}
\end{figure}

Both models achieved an identical micro-average precision of 0.906. The high precision across a wide recall range indicates that the models are able to detect most critical cases while limiting false alarms. This result is particularly important in an emergency triage setting, where missing severe cases may have serious consequences, but excessive false positives may overload clinical workflows.

\subsection{Clinical Error Analysis}

Although the global metrics are high, some misclassifications remain clinically meaningful. The main errors occur between diagnoses that share overlapping symptoms or biomarkers. For example, respiratory and infectious conditions may be confused when patients present common signs such as fever, abnormal respiratory rate, hypoxia, or inflammatory markers. Similarly, neurological conditions such as \texttt{Severe\_Migraine} and \texttt{Epileptic\_Seizure} may overlap when the available observations are insufficiently specific.

These errors suggest that the synthetic dataset is not trivially separable. Instead, it reproduces a degree of ambiguity comparable to emergency triage, where several pathologies may initially present similar clinical profiles. This reinforces the relevance of the generated dataset for evaluating multimodal diagnostic algorithms.

\subsection{Model Interpretability and Feature Importance}

To verify that the models did not rely on spurious statistical artifacts, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were used to analyze feature importance. Figure \ref{fig:shap} presents the global feature importance distribution for a subset of the test data.

\begin{figure*}[t]

The SHAP analysis confirms that the model relies on clinically meaningful variables. Features such as \texttt{Obs\_ChestPain}, \texttt{ECG\_Arrhythmia}, Hypertensive, and HR strongly influence the predictions, which is consistent with the physiological rules embedded in the generation engine. These results support the interpretability of the trained models and provide an additional validation of the synthetic data generation process.

\subsection{Computational Cost}

The Tabular Transformer required 164.0 seconds to train, compared with 34.4 seconds for the MLP baseline. Inference time was also higher for the Transformer, reaching 0.695 seconds compared with 0.302 seconds for the MLP. Although the Transformer achieved the best accuracy, the MLP offers a better trade-off between predictive performance and computational efficiency, making it more suitable for real-time or edge-oriented IoMT deployment.
