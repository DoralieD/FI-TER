\section{Experimental Results}

The evaluation of the proposed multimodal fusion framework was conducted on a 20\% hold-out test set derived from the 500,000 synthetic patient records, and assesses three complementary aspects: the physiological coherence of the generated data, the predictive performance of the three benchmarked models, and the interpretability of the learned decision rules.

A rule-based generator can produce internally consistent data that nonetheless drifts from real clinical populations, so two independent checks are used before evaluating the classifiers. First, Fig.~\ref{fig:corr} reports the strongest absolute correlations among representative vital signs and biomarkers, confirming that dependencies emerge where physiologically expected (e.g., between inflammatory markers such as CRP and Leukocytes, and between cardiovascular indicators such as Troponin and BNP), rather than as random statistical noise.

All differences are statistically significant (p $<$ 0.001), which is expected given the sample sizes involved and is not itself informative; the KS statistic D, bounded in [0,1], is the meaningful quantity. Central tendencies are well reproduced for SpO2, Heart Rate, and Respiratory Rate (D $\leq$ 0.24), indicating that the generator captures realistic average physiology. The largest deviations occur on blood pressure (D $\approx$ 0.45-0.46): the real-world curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:distval} show sharp, narrow peaks at round numbers (110, 120, 130 mmHg), a digit-preference artifact of manual triage measurement rather than a true physiological distribution, whereas the generator produces a smoother continuous spread with a wider variance. This comparison therefore does not indicate that the synthetic data is unrealistic, but rather that it models an idealized continuous physiology instead of the measurement and rounding behavior of human triage staff, a distinction we make explicit rather than obscure.

Across all three models, ROC-AUC and Average Precision remain markedly higher than Accuracy and Macro F1: a macro ROC-AUC of 0.993-0.994 and an Average Precision above 0.89 coexist with Macro F1 scores between 62.6\% and 64.3\%. This gap is a structural consequence of the task rather than an inconsistency: with 24 diagnostic classes, these threshold-independent, macro-averaged metrics aggregate performance over $\binom{24}{2}$ pairwise class separations, most of which are easy (e.g., separating STEMI from Severe Migraine), which inflates the score. Accuracy and Macro F1, in contrast, are computed directly on the hardest decision boundaries at a fixed operating threshold and penalize every misclassification among clinically overlapping conditions equally, regardless of how rare the confusion is. No single model dominates across all four threshold-dependent metrics: the Transformer attains the highest Accuracy, Recall, and ROC-AUC, while XGBoost achieves the highest Precision and Macro F1. This divergence indicates that the Transformer classifies a larger share of majority-class patients correctly, whereas XGBoost is comparatively more conservative and precise on the harder, minority diagnoses that dominate the Macro F1 score; the Transformer's Accuracy advantage over XGBoost (78.3\% vs. 74.6\%) is therefore not a uniform improvement but a shift in error distribution across classes. 

Addressing previous recommendations to streamline visual analysis, the confusion matrices have been structurally compacted (Fig.~\ref{fig:confmat}) to facilitate direct side-by-side comparison. They further confirm that errors are not random: residual misclassifications concentrate on diagnosis pairs that share overlapping symptoms or biomarkers at triage time, such as Asthma Attack versus Anxiety-Induced Hyperventilation, or Severe Migraine versus Epileptic Seizure, rather than being spread uniformly across all 24 classes. This pattern indicates that the models struggle precisely where a human clinician would also require further examinations to disambiguate, which supports the dataset's relevance as a challenging, realistic benchmark rather than a trivially separable one.

To verify that the classifiers rely on genuine physiological signal rather than spurious statistical artifacts, SHAP was applied independently to the neural network and to XGBoost (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}), since these two paradigms compute feature attributions through fundamentally different mechanisms (gradient-based versus tree-structure-based).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\columnwidth]{07a_SHAP_NeuralNetwork.png}
\includegraphics[width=0.48\columnwidth]{07b_SHAP_XGBoost.png}
\caption{Mean absolute SHAP feature importance for the Neural Network (left) and XGBoost (right), showing convergent reliance on clinically meaningful variables.}
\label{fig:shap}
\end{figure}

Comparing the top-8 most influential features of each model shows an overlap of 3/8 variables: Troponin, BNP, and Chest X-Ray finding are ranked among the most important drivers by both models, despite each model also relying on paradigm-specific secondary features (e.g., Creatinine and LVEF for the neural network; PCT, Lactates, and ECG ST-Elevation for XGBoost). This cross-architecture agreement on these clinical drivers is a stronger indicator of genuine rule-learning than either explanation taken alone: it is unlikely that two structurally unrelated models would converge on the same variables by coincidence, so this shared core constitutes evidence that both models learned the intended physiological relationships embedded in the generation engine.

For real-world IoMT deployment, computational efficiency is as important as predictive accuracy, since edge and fog nodes operate under strict memory and energy budgets. As reported in Table~\ref{tab:perf}, the Tabular Transformer's self-attention mechanism, which scales quadratically with the number of features, required 264.3 seconds to train against 40.3 seconds for the MLP and only 15.4 seconds for XGBoost, with inference time following the same trend (0.628 s versus 0.180 s and 0.548 s, respectively). The Transformer's Accuracy and ROC-AUC advantage therefore comes at a training cost roughly 6 to 17 times higher than the other two models, for a Macro F1 that remains below XGBoost's. Consequently, XGBoost offers the best overall accuracy-latency trade-off, followed closely by the MLP, making both substantially more viable than the Transformer for decentralized, edge-oriented IoMT deployment where retraining or on-device inference budgets are constrained.
