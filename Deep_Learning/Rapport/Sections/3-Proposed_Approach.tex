\section{Proposed Approach}

To address the challenges of multimodal IoMT data fusion, the limited availability of accessible medical datasets, and the need for empirically-grounded synthetic data, we designed an end-to-end framework combining a rule-based synthetic data generation algorithm, an external distributional validation step, data preprocessing, three benchmarked classification models, and a cost-sensitive training strategy.

The first component of our framework is a generation algorithm designed to simulate an emergency triage environment. Instead of learning patient distributions directly from real EHRs, the generator explicitly encodes physiological relationships to preserve medical coherence while avoiding privacy constraints. Continuous variables are drawn from truncated normal distributions rather than clipped normal distributions, which prevents the artificial probability mass accumulation at physiological boundaries that a naive \texttt{clip()} operation would otherwise introduce.

The generation follows a two-level pipeline. Level 1 creates the initial triage profile using baseline demographic, vital-sign (e.g., HR, Blood Pressure, SpO2), and observational variables (e.g., chest pain, confusion). Based on these indicators, patients are routed to Level 2 for specialty-specific examinations across Cardiology, Pulmonology, Neurology, and Infectious Diseases. Diagnosis-specific rules are grounded in clinically plausible thresholds; for instance, Troponin levels are sampled around 1800~ng/L for STEMI versus 250~ng/L for NSTEMI, BNP is escalated above 300~pg/mL for Acute Heart Failure, D-Dimers exceed 500~ng/mL under suspicion of Pulmonary Embolism, and Lactates surpass 2.5~mmol/L to characterize Severe Sepsis. This mimics the principle of multimodal healthcare fusion, where heterogeneous data sources are combined to improve diagnostics.

The pipeline covers 24 diagnoses, ranging from time-critical conditions (e.g., STEMI, severe sepsis) to benign overlapping conditions (e.g., anxiety hyperventilation) to introduce clinical ambiguity. To reproduce the sparsity of real-world hospitals, only clinically relevant specialty exams are generated. Missing tests therefore reflect clinical decisions rather than random errors. The output is a sparse multimodal tabular dataset of 500,000 synthetic patient records, approximating the heterogeneous information processed in IoMT-assisted triage systems.

A purely rule-based generator carries the risk of producing data that is internally coherent but not representative of real clinical populations. To mitigate this risk and to provide empirical evidence of clinical realism, we compared the marginal distributions of our synthetic vital signs against an independent, publicly available, real-world reference: an open-access dataset of 143,582 Emergency Department admissions from a large teaching hospital in Isfahan, Iran. Seven shared variables (Age, Heart Rate, Systolic and Diastolic Blood Pressure, Respiratory Rate, Temperature, and SpO2) were aligned by name and unit, and their distributions were compared using kernel density estimation together with a two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test, which quantifies the maximum distance between the empirical cumulative distribution functions of the synthetic and real samples. This procedure provides a transparent, reproducible, and quantified account of how close the generator is from a clinical distribution.

Diagnostic labels are merged into a single global target. Non-performed medical examinations, which previously were imputed with zeros, are now imputed using the per-feature median computed over patients for whom the test was actually performed. This change directly addresses a methodological risk identified during peer review: zero-imputation conflates two distinct pieces of information, namely "the test was not clinically indicated" and "the test returned a null value," which could allow a classifier to shortcut the diagnostic task by learning test-ordering patterns rather than genuine physiological characteristics. Median imputation limits this artificial signal by making a missing value statistically indistinguishable from a typical value of that biomarker. Continuous variables are subsequently standardized to ensure uniform gradient propagation for the neural architectures.

Medical datasets naturally exhibit strong class imbalance. Since classical interpolation techniques risk generating physiologically inconsistent samples when applied to heterogeneous medical variables, our approach avoids post-hoc oversampling. Instead, the generation engine directly controls the occurrence of minority diagnoses. In addition, imbalance is handled during training through a cost-sensitive learning strategy. We utilize a weighted Cross-Entropy loss function, defined for a single patient across $C$ classes as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = - \sum_{i=1}^{C} w_i y_i \log(\hat{y}_i)
\end{equation}
where $y_i$ is the binary indicator of the true class, $\hat{y}_i$ is the predicted probability for class $i$, and $w_i$ is the weight assigned to class $i$. Minority classes receive higher weights, forcing the model to penalize errors on rare, life-threatening conditions. For the tree-based baseline, the same class weights are applied as per-sample weights during training.

The synthetic dataset is used to benchmark three classification models spanning both the deep learning and tree-based tabular paradigms, addressing the recommendation to compare against established machine learning methods.

\textit{Baseline MLP (Early Fusion):} A fully connected network composed of two hidden layers (256 and 128 neurons), representing a classical early-fusion strategy. All modalities are concatenated into a single feature vector before processing. Batch Normalization and Dropout \cite{srivastava2014} are applied to mitigate overfitting. 

\textit{Tabular Transformer (Contextual Fusion):} A Transformer-inspired model adapted to tabular data. Each input feature is projected into a $d_{model}=64$ embedding space. A learnable classification token is added, and the sequence is processed by Transformer encoders using the Scaled Dot-Product Attention mechanism, formulated as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
\end{equation}
where $Q$, $K$, and $V$ represent the query, key, and value matrices, and $d_k$ is the dimension of the key vectors. This contextual fusion allows the model to dynamically adjust the importance of each medical variable based on the patient's context.

\textit{XGBoost (Tree-Based Baseline):} We include a gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble as a third, non-neural baseline. Trees natively partition the feature space along individual biomarker thresholds, which makes them a strong reference model for rule-generated data such as ours. Class imbalance is handled through per-sample weighting rather than an architectural mechanism.

Both neural models are trained using the same experimental protocol with an 80/20 train-test split. We utilize the AdamW optimizer \cite{loshchilov2019} (learning rate of 0.002, weight decay of $1\times 10^{-4}$) and a batch size of 1024 to accelerate training. To ensure convergence, a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler and an Early Stopping mechanism are implemented, restoring the best model weights at the end of training to minimize overfitting. The final evaluation encompasses Accuracy, Precision, Recall, Macro F1-score, ROC-AUC, Precision-Recall curves, computational cost, and SHAP-based interpretability for all three models.
